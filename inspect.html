The Inspector analyses the structure of a number using Google’s open-source numbering data. It cannot see who owns a number, and caller ID can be spoofed — so always judge a call by what the caller asks you to do.

How to read your result

Valid vs. possible

A number is possible when it has the right length for its country. It is valid when its prefixes also match a range the national regulator has opened. Spoofed caller IDs are often possible but invalid — the scammer typed random digits that look plausible.

Line type

Mobile, landline, toll-free, premium-rate, VoIP and shared-cost numbers sit in different prefix ranges. In North America, mobile and landline numbers share the same ranges, so you will see “Landline or mobile”. Elsewhere — the UK, India, Australia and most of Europe — the type is usually exact.

  • Premium-rate numbers charge the caller a high per-minute rate. Never return an unexpected call to one.
  • VoIP numbers can be created online in minutes. Plenty of honest businesses use them; so do call centres running scams.
  • Toll-free numbers are free to dial, but criminals love spoofing them because they look official.

The four formats

E.164 (for example +12125550142) is the global standard used by phone systems, CRMs and SMS platforms — store numbers this way. International adds spaces for humans. National is how locals write it. The RFC 3966 link (tel:+1-212-555-0142) makes a number tappable on websites.

North American red flags we check

  • 555-0100 to 555-0199 — reserved for fiction; can never ring a real person.
  • 900 numbers — pay-per-call.
  • Caribbean and Atlantic codes such as 876 (Jamaica), 809/829/849 (Dominican Republic), 473 (Grenada) or 284 (British Virgin Islands) — they look domestic but are international, which is exactly what one-ring scams exploit.
  • N11 exchanges like 911 or 411 in the middle of a number — impossible for a real subscriber, a common sign of spoofing.
  • Unassigned area codes — not in our list of 450 active codes.

Number looks fine but the call felt wrong?

Trust the feeling. Scammers routinely borrow real, valid numbers — even your own bank’s. Run the Scam Call Checker, hang up, and call the organisation back on a number you find yourself.

For developers
