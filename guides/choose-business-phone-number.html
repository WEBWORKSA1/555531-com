Start with who calls you

Ask two questions: where are your customers, and how do they find your number? A plumber whose customers search “plumber near me” needs a local number. A national e-commerce brand whose number sits in an order confirmation may prefer toll-free. A business advertising on radio or vehicles needs something memorable.

The options

Local numbers

Match your area code to your service area. People are more likely to answer and return calls from a familiar area code, and local numbers are cheap — often included free with a cloud phone plan. You can hold several local numbers for different cities and route them all to one team.

Toll-free numbers

800, 833, 844, 855, 866, 877 and 888 in North America. Callers pay nothing; you pay for inbound minutes. They signal national presence and work for support lines. Toll-free numbers are portable between providers, and “800” numbers remain the most recognisable and most sought-after.

Vanity numbers

A number that spells a word or forms a pattern — 1-8XX-FLOWERS style or repeating digits. Memorability is the point: vanity numbers tend to outperform random digits in broadcast, outdoor and vehicle advertising where people can’t click. Expect to pay more for the best combinations.

Porting your existing number

If customers already know your number, keep it. Number portability lets you move it to a new provider. Never cancel your old service before the port completes, and keep your account number and PIN handy — mismatched details are the top cause of delays.

Will people pick up?

With tens of billions of robocalls a year, many people don’t answer unknown numbers. Before choosing a provider, ask about:

  • Branded caller ID showing your business name and logo.
  • STIR/SHAKEN A-level attestation on outbound calls — see our spoofing guide.
  • Text messaging registration. In the US, texting from local numbers requires 10DLC brand and campaign registration; toll-free texting requires verification.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Using a personal mobile number as your business line — you can’t hand it to staff or sell it with the business.
  • Choosing an area code far from your customers to look “big”.
  • Buying a number without checking its history; recycled numbers may already be flagged as spam. Run it through the Number Inspector and ask the provider for a fresh one if it was previously used for telemarketing.
  • Letting the number lapse after a rebrand — it may be reassigned to someone else.
