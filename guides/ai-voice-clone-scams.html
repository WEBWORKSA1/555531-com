What changed

The “grandparent scam” is decades old: a caller pretends to be a relative in trouble — arrested, hospitalised, stranded abroad — and asks for urgent, secret money. What has changed is the voice. Consumer AI tools can now produce a convincing copy of someone’s voice from a short clip taken from social media, a voicemail greeting or a video. Researchers describe the best clones as effectively indistinguishable from the real person over a phone line.

The scale is real. A 2026 Gallup and Stop Scams Alliance survey of more than 5,000 US adults found that roughly one in eight successful scams in 2025 involved AI or deepfakes. The FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center began tracking “AI-related” complaints as a category for 2025 and logged more than 22,000 of them, with older adults carrying a disproportionate share of losses. McAfee’s global survey found about one in four adults had experienced a voice-clone scam or knew someone who had.

How the call usually unfolds

  1. The hook: a short, emotional burst in the cloned voice — “Mum, I’ve had an accident, please help.”
  2. The handover: a second person takes over — a “lawyer”, “police officer” or “kidnapper” — who explains what money is needed. Clones are best at short emotional phrases, so the scammer keeps the cloned voice brief.
  3. The pressure: it must happen now, and you must not tell anyone (“she’ll lose her job”, “the judge will refuse bail”).
  4. The payment: cash collected by a courier, gift cards, a crypto ATM or a wire transfer — all hard to reverse.

Why a family safe word works

AI can copy a voice; it can’t copy a secret it has never heard. A safe word is a word or short phrase agreed in person by your family and never posted, texted or emailed. In a real emergency, the caller can say it. A scammer can’t. The FBI and consumer groups now recommend it as the single most effective defence.

Choosing a good safe word

  • Random and specific: “pineapple lighthouse”, not a pet’s name or birthday that appears online.
  • Easy to remember under stress — practise it once.
  • Shared only in person. Write it on the wallet card in our free family kit, not in a group chat.
  • Change it if you think it has leaked.

What to do if you get the call

  1. Ask for the safe word. If they don’t know it, dodge it or get angry, hang up.
  2. Hang up and call back on the number you already have for that person — not the number that called you. Caller ID can be faked.
  3. Contact someone nearby: a partner, sibling or friend who can check on the person.
  4. Never pay by gift card, crypto, wire or courier because a caller told you to.
  5. Report it, even if you didn’t lose money. See where to report.

Reduce your voice footprint

  • Use your phone’s default voicemail greeting instead of a recording of your own voice.
  • Limit public videos where you speak at length, or restrict them to friends.
  • Let unknown callers speak first; avoid saying “yes” to open questions from strangers.

Businesses: the CEO voice scam

The same technique targets companies: a “director” calls finance staff to approve an urgent transfer. Require call-back verification on a known number and two-person approval for any payment change requested by phone — no exceptions for seniority.
