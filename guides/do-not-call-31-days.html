The short version

In both the United States and Canada, adding your number to the national do-not-call list does not silence sales calls overnight. Telemarketers covered by the rules have up to 31 days from your registration date to remove your number from their lists. In the US, covered telemarketers must also download the registry at least once every 31 days. After that window, a sales call from a covered company is a potential violation you can report.

Why 31 days?

The registry is a list, not a blocker. Nothing in the phone network checks it when a call is placed. Instead, telemarketers are legally required to “scrub” their calling lists against it. When the US registry launched in 2003 they had three months to do so; since January 2005 the window has been 31 days. Canada adopted the same 31-day period for its National DNCL. The idea is practical: give legitimate businesses a monthly cycle to update their systems.

What the registry stops

  • Sales calls from businesses that follow the law and have no relationship with you.
  • In the US, it covers both mobile and landline personal numbers. Registration doesn’t expire unless the number is disconnected and reassigned, or you remove it.
  • In Canada, you can register home, mobile, fax and VoIP numbers; registrations are permanent.

What the registry does not stop

  • Scammers. Criminals ignore the list. If a caller is threatening you or asking for gift cards, the registry was never going to help — blocking and hanging up will.
  • Political calls, charities and surveys (US rules; Canada has its own exemptions for charities, political parties, pollsters and newspapers seeking subscriptions).
  • Companies you already do business with. In the US, an “established business relationship” lasts 18 months after a purchase or three months after an inquiry — unless you ask them to stop.
  • Informational calls such as appointment reminders, delivery notices or fraud alerts from your own bank.

The company-specific request: your secret weapon

Even an exempt company must honour a direct request. If a business you have dealt with calls, say clearly: “Please put my number on your internal do-not-call list.” Note the date, time and the name of the person. In the US, the company must honour that request; ignoring it is a violation on its own, regardless of the national registry.

How to use the 31 days well

  1. Register every personal line at DoNotCall.gov (US) or lnnte-dncl.gc.ca (Canada).
  2. Turn on call filtering on your phone and through your carrier, because scam calls won’t stop on their own.
  3. Start a call log. Date, time, number shown, company named, what they wanted. Complaints with details are far more useful.
  4. Set a reminder for day 31 — our Quiet Line tool creates a calendar file for you.

Day 32: filing a complaint that counts

If a covered telemarketer calls after the 31 days, file a complaint. In the US, use DoNotCall.gov; in Canada, use the DNCL website. Include the date and time, the number on your caller ID, any callback number you were given, the company name and whether it was a recorded message. Regulators don’t resolve individual complaints, but they use the patterns to pick enforcement targets and to share numbers with call-blocking providers.

Robocalls with a pre-recorded sales pitch are generally illegal in the US unless you gave the company written permission — whether or not you are on the registry.

Common questions

Do I have to re-register every few years?

No. US and Canadian registrations are permanent unless the number is disconnected or you remove it.

Can I register my business line?

The US registry is for personal numbers; business-to-business calls aren’t covered. Canada allows registration of any number type, but business-to-business calls are exempt.

I removed my number by mistake. What happens?

Removal takes effect the next day, and telemarketers have up to 31 days to pick up the change. Re-register as soon as possible.
