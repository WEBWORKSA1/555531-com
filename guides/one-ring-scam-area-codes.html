How the scam works

“Wangiri” is Japanese for “one ring and cut”. An automated system dials thousands of numbers, lets each ring once, and hangs up before you can answer. The goal is to make you call back. The number it shows is international or premium-rate, and the scammers share the revenue the call generates — often by keeping you on hold or playing a recording that sounds like a real call centre.

Why North America is especially exposed

The United States and Canada share the +1 country code with about two dozen other countries and territories in the Caribbean and Atlantic. Their numbers look exactly like domestic ones: three-digit area code, seven-digit number. Without knowing the codes, a call from (876) xxx-xxxx looks no different from one from (867) xxx-xxxx — but 876 is Jamaica, and 867 is northern Canada.

Codes for US territories such as Puerto Rico (787, 939), the US Virgin Islands (340), Guam (671), the Northern Mariana Islands (670) and American Samoa (684) are domestic for US callers, though rates can still vary by plan. Plenty of honest people live in all these places — the rule is simply: don’t call back numbers you don’t recognise.

Red flags

  • One ring, often at night, then silence and no voicemail.
  • Several missed calls from similar numbers in a short time.
  • A voicemail urging you to call back about a prize, a parcel or a family emergency.
  • A text asking you to call a number to “confirm” something you never ordered.

If you already called back

  1. Hang up immediately. Charges usually accrue per minute.
  2. Check your next bill for international or premium charges and dispute any you didn’t knowingly authorise with your carrier.
  3. Report the number to your carrier and regulator — see where to report.
  4. Ask your carrier to block international or premium-rate calling if you don’t need it.
